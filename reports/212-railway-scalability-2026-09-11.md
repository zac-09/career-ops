**Gaps, ranked by how much they actually cost:**

1. **Postgres depth — the load-bearing gap.** The JD does not say "SQL experience", it says *"Deep expertise in Postgres… know how Postgres itself behaves at scale"* and then puts *"what happens when Postgres becomes the bottleneck"* directly into the interview rubric. Isaac's CV says the word "SQL" once. **Mitigation:** this is the one thing worth studying before applying, not after. Two to three focused evenings: MVCC and how bloat/vacuum actually bite; transaction isolation levels and what each one buys and costs; index types and when a partial or covering index changes the plan; connection pooling (PgBouncer, transaction vs session mode) and why a Node.js service with too many connections kills a database; partitioning and retention for append-heavy usage-event tables; `SELECT … FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` for queue-in-Postgres patterns; logical replication and read-replica lag as a correctness hazard. Then put those decisions **in the prework doc with reasons**, which converts study into evidence.
2. **GraphQL — clean absence.** Not on the CV, not in profile.yml. Do not claim it. Honest line: "I've built REST and gRPC APIs with versioned contracts; GraphQL is a schema-design problem I haven't shipped, and schema-first design is how I already work" (the protobuf/USSD contract story, #127, is the credible bridge).
3. **Temporal / durable execution.** Adjacent experience is real (Pub/Sub, Kafka, NATS) but the JD's phrase is "exactly-once semantics and durability actually matter". **Mitigation:** spend one evening on Temporal's model — workflows vs activities, determinism constraints on workflow code, replay from event history, activity retries and heartbeats, idempotency keys at the activity boundary — and put a Temporal workflow in the prework design for the billing run. That single move turns a gap into a demonstrated understanding.
4. **Billing / Stripe / money movement.** No amount of study substitutes for having run a billing system, so **do not fake it — pick billing as the prework domain instead.** The JD offers a choice of domains; choosing the metering-and-billing one is how Isaac shows the thinking he can't show on the CV. Read Stripe's idempotency-key and webhook-reliability docs so the vocabulary is right.
5. **ClickHouse.** One evening of reading: why a MergeTree beats Postgres for high-cardinality event ingestion, materialised views for pre-aggregation, and the sharp edge that matters here — ClickHouse is eventually consistent on dedup (`ReplacingMergeTree` merges are asynchronous), so it is the wrong place to hold the ledger of record. Knowing *that* is worth more than knowing the syntax.
6. **"Made it handle 10x" with a number.** Isaac genuinely lacks this. The closest honest framing is the 2M-record Firestore read path and the two-datastore sync under parallel live traffic. **Do not invent a multiplier.** Say: "My scale work has been correctness-under-load and cost-per-read rather than a raw throughput multiple — here is the hardest one."
7. **TypeScript not on cv.md.** profile.yml asserts 5+ years; the CV's Skills line says JavaScript. Surfaced as "JavaScript/TypeScript" in the tailored PDF (truthful per profile.yml). Recommend Isaac add TypeScript to cv.md so cv-sync stays clean — it has now cost him keyword alignment on several TS-titled reqs.
8. **Timezone width.** UTC+3 against an SF-anchored team is the widest offset in the pipeline. Not disqualifying in an async, two-meetings-a-week culture, but it will come up. Own it first.

**The realistic band.** A $100M Series B infra company in San Francisco paying "great salary" plus "strong equity grants" for a senior backend engineer is paying US-market money to US engineers — comfortably $180K–$250K base-plus-equity territory. **Comp risk: HIGH-VARIANCE, not high-downside.** The floor risk is real but modest — a company competing globally for a senior engineer who can architect its billing system is not going to anchor at Ugandan market rate and expect a yes. ### Recommended prework: the usage metering and billing pipeline

Railway offers a choice of prework domains. **Pick the billing one** — not the observability one. Isaac's weakest evidenced area is money movement, and the prework is the only place in the process where he can demonstrate the thinking without needing the résumé line. Choosing the stream-processing option would play to strength but leave the actual gap untouched.

Design a per-second CPU/RAM metering and billing pipeline for millions of workloads. Structure it so the doc answers the rubric's five questions before they're asked:

1. **Ingestion and the polling question.** Agents emit usage events; push to a durable log (Kafka or equivalent), not a polling loop. State the reason explicitly — polling stops being enough the moment your poll interval can't keep up with event arrival without either dropping resolution or hammering the source. Handle late and out-of-order events with watermarks and a bounded lateness window; say what happens to an event that arrives after the window closes (side-channel to a repair queue, never silently dropped).
2. **Not losing data while streaming.** At-least-once from the agent with a monotonic sequence number per workload; dedup on `(workload_id, sequence)` at the aggregation boundary. Say plainly why dedup lives in the aggregation layer and not in ClickHouse: `ReplacingMergeTree` merges asynchronously, so the OLAP store is where you *query* usage, never where you *establish* it. **This is Isaac's #167 story expressed as architecture** — make the connection explicit in the doc, it is the credibility bridge.
3. **Cardinality.** Aggregate to per-workload-per-minute buckets before the rating engine ever sees a row; ClickHouse MergeTree with a sensible ORDER BY and materialised views for the pre-aggregates; keep raw events on a short retention and roll up beyond it. Name the cardinality bomb you're avoiding (per-second × per-workload × per-resource is the trap).
4. **Correctness in the rating and charge path.** The billing run as a **Temporal workflow** — deterministic workflow code, activities for each external effect, replayable from event history, so a mid-run crash resumes rather than double-bills. Idempotency keys on every Stripe call derived from `(customer, period, attempt)` so a provider timeout retried is never a second charge. A reconciliation job that diffs the ledger against Stripe's view daily and alerts on drift rather than auto-correcting. Explicitly answer "what happens when a step fails halfway through": the invoice is built in a staging table, finalised in one transaction, and the charge is a separate activity with its own idempotency key.
5. **When Postgres becomes the bottleneck.** This is the question they will push hardest on, so answer it before they ask. Postgres holds the ledger and the invoices — the things that need transactions and audit — not the raw event firehose. When write load climbs: partition the append-heavy tables by time, keep indexes narrow on the write path, pool connections through PgBouncer in transaction mode (and say why a Node.js service left unpooled exhausts `max_connections` long before CPU), move read-heavy reporting to a replica while being explicit that replica lag makes it unsafe for anything the invoice depends on, and only then consider sharding by customer. Name the failure you're most afraid of — vacuum falling behind on a high-churn table and bloat degrading the write path — and how you'd see it coming.

Close the doc with a short "what I'd validate first" section and an explicit open-questions list. Railway's culture section rewards judgment over process; a doc that flags its own uncertainties reads as more senior than one that pretends to certainty.
